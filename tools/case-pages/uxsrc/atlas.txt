# 02 / The problem || The difficult part is the handoff.
h: The scenario
p: A product lead asks an agent to compare Northstar, Relay and Fieldwork, prepare a report, and draft an email for eight reviewers. These products, sources and recipients are fictional. The scenario pushes the question: what should the person know before sharing the result?
p: In ATLAS, each agent is a simulation with a particular responsibility.
p: **Term to know:** An **artifact** is a saved piece of work, such as extracted notes, a comparison or a report.
table: Handoff risk | Question the interface must answer
tr: Unclear scope | What will happen before I give up control?
tr: Vague consent | Which content goes to which recipients, and can it be recalled?
tr: Changing content | Does my earlier approval still apply after an edit?
tr: Interrupted execution | What stopped, what survived, and where can I resume?
note: The design opportunity: :: Make scope, ownership and the next human decision visible throughout delegated work.
h: An assumed risk journey
p: Brief the system > delegate > lose context > decide whether to intervene > reconstruct interrupted work.
h: What I could investigate within the constraint
p: With no paid services or live integrations, I focused on the interaction contract - What the user can

# 03 / Secondary research || Start with what the evidence supports.
table: Published guidance | Interpretation for ATLAS
tr: **Microsoft / CHI 2019 [1]** Human-AI interaction guidance addresses capability clarity, correction and user control. | Expose the fixed plan and available interventions. Test whether a user can predict what will happen next.
tr: **Google PAIR [2]** Control should fit the task; users need ways to adjust outputs and understand what their feedback changes. | Keep edit, reject and clarification distinct. A local approval changes this proposal's state; it does not train a model.
tr: **NIST AI RMF 1.0, Appendix C [3]** Human oversight roles and responsibilities need explicit definition; outcomes depend on context. | Name the specialist responsible for each stage and the person responsible for each consequential decision.
h: Synthesis: three questions worth testing
rows: Scope: | can a person explain the plan before it runs?
rows: Consent: | can they identify the exact action they are authorizing?
rows: Recovery: | can they tell completed work from work that still needs to run?

# 04 / Empathize || Two roles. Different reasons to intervene.
p: These provisional personas define whom I would recruit and what I would investigate. They are role hypotheses.
card: Product lead :: **Job to support.** Delegate a comparison, assess its report and decide whether to share it. :: **Assumed need.** Inspect scope, sources, recipients and unresolved questions without watching every stage. :: **Question to test.** Does the lead need all recipients and the full payload together, or a different review sequence?
card: Operations reviewer :: **Job to support.** Set task boundaries and investigate work that has stopped. :: **Assumed need.** Distinguish prepared, approved, blocked and completed work and finally find a justified next step. :: **Question to test.** Can the reviewer explain why retry is unavailable and what must change before it becomes eligible?
h: The shared job
note: :: Help me delegate the work while keeping enough context to make the decisions that remain mine.
p: The lead's decision is about **the content and its reach.**
p: The reviewer's decision is about **execution and recovery.**
p: ATLAS connects both to the same task, so approval, permissions, saved work and history describe one underlying state.
h: What would change these personas
p: I would recruit people who already delegate multi-step knowledge work, ask them to walk through a recent task, and compare their actual responsibilities. If one person owns both roles, I would test whether the separate views help or add navigation.

# 05 / Define || Make the transfer of control explicit.
quote: :: How might we help knowledge workers supervise delegated work, make informed decisions before consequential actions, and recover without losing completed work?
p: I translated the four handoff risks into interaction requirements. These criteria specify what the prototype must do.
table: How might we... | Design response | Behavior to verify
tr: Show the next action before it happens? | An inspectable plan, stage ownership and optional plan checkpoint. | A requested plan checkpoint holds execution with zero outputs.
tr: Keep consent attached to exact scope? | Consequence Preview with recipients, content, shared data and reversibility. | Every simulated communication requires review and approval of the current revision.
tr: Grant autonomy without granting blanket consent? | Task-specific permissions, checked separately from exact approval. | A revoked permission blocks the next affected operation.
tr: Make failure resumable? | Save work per stage and expose retry eligibility. | A failed comparison retains earlier outputs; retry resumes at that stage.
h: Design principles
list: Explain the next action before asking for a decision.
list: Keep approval specific.
list: Preserve useful work when execution stops.
list: Name the responsible specialist.
list: Keep the simulation boundary visible.

# 06 / Ideate || Where I put the friction.
p: The comparison below explains the trade-offs behind the implemented patterns.
table: Design choice | Why it fits this use case | Cost to watch
tr: **Persistent workspace** Compared with a chat-only history. | Tasks, statuses and decisions remain available as stable objects to inspect. | Nine destinations can create navigation overhead.
tr: **Exact payload review** Compared with a summary-only approval. | The decision can point to specific recipients and content. | Eight recipients plus a full report can encourage skim-reading.
tr: **Required action gate** Compared with approving every preparation step. | The communication boundary always needs a decision; plan review can be added. | People may misunderstand which checkpoints are optional.
tr: **Stage-level recovery** Compared with restarting the whole task. | Retained outputs make recovery inspectable and avoid repeated completed steps. | Dependencies and retry eligibility need explanation.
h: What I prioritized
rows: Core: | delegation, visible execution, exact approval, task permissions and recovery.
rows: Supporting: | specialist views, workflow dependencies, event history, onboarding and local preferences.
rows: Beyond the prototype: | dynamic AI planning, live integrations and a shared organization workspace.

h: The core flow
flow: 1 / Brief | Objective, context, tools and task boundaries.
flow: 2 / Plan review | Inspect the plan; request a human gate if needed.
flow: 3 / Execution | Five fixed stages, owned by four specialists.
flow: 4 / Exact-action review | Review recipients, payload and consequences.
flow: 5 / Record | Save the decision, exact payload and artifacts.
p: **Exception paths:** pause > inspect > resume; failure > inspect saved work > eligible retry; denial > resolve the boundary > eligible retry. Rejection and cancellation stop future work while retaining prepared outputs.

# 10 / Solution: task states || Prepared is not approved.
p: A progress bar answers how much selected work is prepared.
figs: 05 | My Tasks detail: all work can be prepared while the final action still awaits approval.
table: State | What it means / what happens next
tr: Draft | The brief exists; planning has not started.
tr: Planning | The fixed stages are available to inspect or submit for plan review.
tr: Awaiting approval | A requested plan gate or mandatory exact-action gate holds execution.
tr: Running | The next selected, permitted stage can advance.
tr: Paused | Execution is held until the person resumes.
tr: Failed | An error or denial stopped progress; inspect saved work and retry eligibility.
tr: Cancelled | Future work is stopped; existing outputs remain.
tr: Partially completed | Prepared work remains after omitted tools or rejection.
tr: Completed | The run has finished and its required decisions are resolved.

# 11 / Solution: consequence preview || Approve this. Exactly this.
p: Consequence Preview brings together the agent, parent task, affected resource, all recipients, exact subject and body, shared data, possible consequences, reversibility and required permission.
p: The default email has eight demo recipients.

h: The decision rules
rows: Review the current revision. | Approval stays unavailable until its exact-review box is checked.
rows: Check current permission. | Enabling communication does not bypass exact approval.
rows: Explain reversibility. | A real email could be forwarded and cannot reliably be recalled. This demo sends nothing.
p: **Why:** a specific decision can be explained and recorded later.

# 12 / Solution: decision branches || A new draft needs a new yes.

h: Clarification holds the action
p: Resolving the question returns the proposal to fresh exact review. It does not authorize communication.

# 13 / Solution: autonomy boundaries || Permission is not consent.
p: Permission answers what a task may attempt. Approval answers whether this exact consequential action may proceed.
figs: 11 | Permissions overview crop. Boundaries belong to the selected task; the complete permission model is summarized below.
table: Boundary | Current prototype behavior
tr: Read permission | Allows fictional fixture reading; denial can stop a read step.
tr: Draft creation | Allows preparation of local notes, comparisons, reports and drafts.
tr: External communication | Requires current permission plus exact revision review and approval. Records a simulation only.
tr: Modify / Delete / Paid action | Stored settings for future scope. The current pipeline does not implement these Operations and they remain blocked even when enabled.
p: Every future operation checks current boundaries. Revocation blocks the next affected step and preserves earlier work. Restoring the required boundary can make a failed step eligible for retry.
p: **Trade-off:** unsupported switches communicate the broader model but may imply capabilities the prototype lacks. I would test that interpretation before expanding the permission set.

# 14 / Solution: failure recovery || Resume from the break.
p: A failure should reveal what stopped and what remains available before offering another attempt.
list: The comparison-error scenario stops after research and extraction.
list: The task is Failed with two saved artifacts.
list: Recovery Center exposes the failed stage, retained work and retry eligibility.
list: An eligible retry continues at comparison without duplicating earlier outputs.
figs3: 12 | **1 / Failure.** Comparison stops. Two earlier outputs remain available. ;; 13 | **2 / Inspect.** Saved research and extracted notes can be inspected before retry. ;; 14 | **3 / Retry.** Five outputs are prepared. Exact-action review is still pending.
note: Recovery has conditions. :: A permission, dependency or fixed simulation limit can keep retry blocked. A retry button is only useful when the next attempt is allowed to proceed.
p: **Why:** preserved artifacts make recovery inspectable.
p: **Limit:** these are local fixture outputs. The prototype does not resolve changed external data, duplicate real-world side effects or uncertain partial execution by a live agent.

# 15 / Solution: history & saved work || Give the decision a receipt.
p: Activity History is the Agent Flight Recorder. Task filters narrow the record - event details explain plans, attempts, denials, edits, decisions and retries. Approved payloads remain saved as snapshots, so later edits do not overwrite what was previously approved.
figs: 15 | Activity History overview crop. This is a separate sample task, illustrating attributed events and filters.
rows: Saved work travels with the task. | Outputs remain inspectable and downloadable after failure, rejection or cancellation. They are marked as simulated.
rows: A useful limit. | The log lives in browser storage. It is inspectable product behavior, not a tamper-proof organizational audit trail.
figs: 16 | Task history detail. The earlier exact approved payload is retained beneath its event.
p: **Why:** supervision needs both the current state and the explanation of how it got there.
p: **Trade-off:** a full log can overwhelm; filters and payload disclosure keep the overview compact while retaining detail.

# 18 / Evaluate: iteration & checks || The audit changed the product.
p: These are documented implementation and interaction fixes.
table: Issue found | Change and supporting check
tr: Fleet status named the coordinator while another specialist owned the active stage. | Attributed status and participating tasks to the responsible specialist. Covered by ownership tests.
tr: A stale browser tab could overwrite a task created elsewhere. | Read the latest saved workspace before updates and allocate unique task IDs. Provider regression checks preserved both tasks.
tr: Clarification copy called every entered address fictional. | Changed it to explain that addresses are used only in the local simulation. Retested with an edited address.

# 19 / Outcome & next validation || A working oversight model. A user-value hypothesis.
p: The prototype connects nine screens, four simulated specialists and five fixed stages. It preserves task state across refresh, holds consequential actions for exact approval and retains earlier outputs during recovery. No usability improvement, adoption lift or business result was measured.
h: What I would test next
p: I would run an initial six to eight moderated sessions with intended product and operations users who already delegate multi-step work. This would be a formative study to locate misunderstandings, not a statistically representative performance benchmark.
table: Task / hypothesis | What I would observe | What would prompt a change
tr: **Delegate and review a plan.** Visible scope supports prediction. | Ask the person to explain what will happen next before execution. | They miss a selected tool or misunderstand an optional checkpoint.
tr: **Inspect an email with a planted discrepancy.** Exact review supports informed consent. | Record whether they notice an unexpected recipient or changed content, without prompting. | They approve despite missing the discrepancy.
tr: **Edit, clarify or reject a proposal.** The branches have distinct meanings. | Ask whether an older approval authorizes the new revision and what the current action may do. | They expect automatic approval or continued execution after rejection.
tr: **Recover from comparison failure.** Retained work supports recovery. | Observe whether they find the two saved artifacts and retry at the failed stage. | They restart unnecessarily or expect blocked retry to work.
p: I would report actual participant counts, observed errors, interventions, task completion and comprehension in the study context, alongside the design changes they led to. Timing would be descriptive at this sample size. A later comparison would be needed to claim improvement over another approach.

# 20 / Reflection & sources || The interface can hold a boundary. People still have to understand it.
p: ATLAS moved my attention from the output alone to the contract around it: what was permitted, what was reviewed, what changed and what survives. The most useful documented correction was ownership. A polished status card still misleads if it names the wrong specialist.
p: My next design priority is the density of exact-action review, especially on mobile. I would test information order and disclosure while keeping recipients, changed content and consequences visible enough to support a deliberate decision.
h: What production would require
p: Browser storage can be cleared; closing the browser stops the simulation. Real execution needs authenticated identity, server-enforced permissions, durable jobs, secure integrations, reliable handling of repeated actions, and retained audit records. Dynamic plans introduce uncertainty the fixed pipeline does not test.
