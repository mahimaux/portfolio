# Backstory || The phone was in the way.
p: A woman on a crowded bus may have a phone in her bag and a reason to leave it there. On a quiet walk to the market, she may have the phone in hand and weak coverage. I wanted to design for both moments, starting with what it takes to ask for help.
h: Understanding the users
p: **Primary research.** The project materials reference interviews but provide no participant count, interview guide or records. I use the published survey and desk research below as evidence. The personas represent scenarios I would validate with commuters in Bengaluru and Sullia.
label: Secondary research
card: Published public-bus study // 614 valid survey responses :: 422 from Bengaluru and 192 from Ahmedabad. The study linked safety perceptions on buses to anxiety, bus-stop conditions and the wider travel experience. :: _Verma et al., Transportation Research Procedia (2020)_
card: Urban and rural mobility // Infrastructure shapes the trip :: The World Bank points to lighting and walkable routes in cities, and limited transport options and poor road conditions in rural areas. I treated weak coverage as a scenario to test in Sullia. :: _World Bank, Gender Responsive Urban Mobility in India (2022) · World Bank, Rural and Peri-Urban Roads_
p: **Global context.** WHO estimates nearly one in three women have experienced physical or sexual intimate partner violence or non-partner sexual violence in their lifetime. This figure covers a broader context than commuting.
small: World Health Organization, global prevalence estimates
h: Product benchmark
p: invisaWear already pairs discreet jewellery with a phone over Bluetooth. My opportunity was a thumb-operated ring paired with three distinct ways to respond during a commute.
small: invisaWear, product and Bluetooth FAQ
note: Evidence to design :: The bus study is about perceived safety. I used it to select scenarios and design constraints. Whether a ring feels discreet and usable in those scenarios remains a question for primary research.

# Interviews and insights || Personas
p: Two research-informed composites show how the same safety task changes with the commute.
card: Urban commuter // Priya Sharma :: 27 · Marketing professional · Bengaluru, Karnataka :: Takes public transport between meetings and sometimes comes home late. :: **Pain points.** Crowded or late trips can feel unsafe. A visible panic device or taking out a phone can draw attention. Menus cost time when she wants to act quickly. :: **Needs.** A gesture she can use with one hand, a ring she would wear as jewellery, and control over whether to call, share location or trigger SOS.
card: Rural commuter // Kavitha Devi :: 38 · Farmer · Sullia, Karnataka :: Walks to nearby markets and fields. Some stretches have few people, poor lighting and intermittent coverage. :: **Pain points.** Help may be farther away. Coverage can drop. A tool that assumes English, confidence with apps or a multistep setup can be hard to use. :: **Needs.** A trigger she can find by touch, setup in a language she reads, and a clear indication of what the app can send when coverage is weak.
note: Shared job :: Get someone to help me when I need it. Priya’s scenario tests discretion in a crowd. Kavitha’s tests how much the emergency interaction assumes about the phone, the network and reading under pressure.
small: Names, ages, jobs and scenarios are composite persona details.

# Interviews and insights || Themes
rows: Discretion | Make the device and gesture inconspicuous enough to use in a crowd.
rows: Accessibility | Use touch for the trigger, start setup with language, and keep the app readable at a glance.
rows: Proportionate response | Give uneasy, worried and endangered commuters different actions.
rows: Readiness | Configure the ring and choose trusted contacts before leaving.
rows: Connectivity | Store what can be stored locally and be clear about what can be delivered.

# Problem statement || Asking for help can be its own obstacle.
p: An emergency interaction can assume a free hand, a visible screen, a good connection and time to decide. A crowded commute can take away the first two. A rural walk can take away the connection. Both make the cost of extra steps apparent.
quote: How might we :: How might we design an intuitive, accessible safety solution that helps women in urban and rural areas feel more secure on their commutes, while accounting for their different needs and keeping it easy and reliable to use?
small: **Design test.** Could someone start an SOS by touch with a hand at their side? Could they tell whether help had actually been notified once they could check their phone?

# Wearable + SOS || User Flow
p: The commuter can start on the ring or demonstrate the same hold on Home in the phone prototype.
flow: Ring trigger | Find the palm-facing panel by touch.
flow: Home hold pad | A second entry point when the phone is accessible.
flow: Hold for 2 seconds | The hold arms SOS. Releasing early resets it.
flow: Silent confirmation window | 5 seconds to cancel on the paired phone.
flow: Cancel | Return to Home. No alert has gone out.
flow: Countdown expires | SOS starts automatically.
flow: Active SOS | The phone shows alert and location status.
flow: I’m safe | Stand down, stop the alarm and send an all-clear.
note: Emergency confirmation :: The hold confirms intent. The countdown gives an accidental trigger a way out. A deliberate alert requires no second tap.

# Wearable + SOS / User flow || Live Location & Alerts
p: At the end of the countdown, the concept starts the local and remote actions in parallel.
flow: Paired phone starts SOS | Uses the configured recipients and location.
card: Trusted contacts :: Share live location through the available data channel.
card: Nearby HalO users :: Send the proposed community alert where available.
card: Ring :: Start the alarm and flash in the concept.
label: When connectivity changes
table: Condition | Proposed behaviour
tr: Mobile data available | Use app-based live sharing and show progress by recipient or channel.
tr: SMS coverage only | Explore an SMS alert to saved contacts with the latest available location. This would send a snapshot.
tr: No data or SMS service | Communicate that the remote alert cannot be sent. A cached map or local alarm does not establish delivery.
note: Shared dependency :: Parallel actions avoid waiting for each other. The phone and available communication channels still determine which remote actions can complete.

# Wearable + SOS / Ring & SOS UI || The Hold & Cancel
p: A bag strap can press a ring. The activation needs to feel deliberate when the user means it.
figs: 05 | **Hold to arm.** The app stand-in fills a progress arc. Releasing before the threshold resets the gesture. ;; 06 | **Cancel in silence.** The countdown tells the commuter when the alert will go out and gives an accidental trigger a way back.
note: Why two seconds, then five? :: The hold is meant to filter brief contact. The cancel window gives the commuter time to recover from an accidental hold. It also delays an intentional alert. Both timings need testing on a physical ring.
small: The phone prototype demonstrates the timing. Whether the wearer can perceive confirmation with the phone in a bag is a hardware research question.

# Wearable + SOS / Ring & SOS UI || Active SOS & Stand-Down
p: Once SOS starts, the interface shifts from asking for input to showing what is happening.
figs: 07 | **SOS active.** Large status text, icons and an elapsed timer make the simulated actions easy to scan. ;; 08 | **Stand-down.** “I’m safe” leads to the all-clear screen. The prototype shows the alarm off and contacts notified.

# Key takeaways || Key Takeaways
p: The concept is a wearable and an interactive walkthrough. The next decision is whether the handoff earns enough trust to pilot.
label: 01 / What did I learn?
card: Start with the hand. :: When the phone is hard to reach, simplifying its screens only helps once the commuter can use it. The wearable moves the first action.
card: Make waiting visible. :: A cancel window can prevent a false alarm and delay a real one. The timing should earn its place through testing.
card: Design for delivery. :: An alert starting, an alert leaving the phone and someone receiving it are different events. Each needs a distinct status.
label: 02 / What’s next?
card: Run primary research. :: Interview urban and rural commuters about recent journeys, language needs and what “discreet” means to them. Include trusted contacts.
card: Test the physical gesture. :: Use a test ring, simulated scenarios and consented time pressure. Compare hold and cancel timings, including hands-busy and accidental presses.
card: Review privacy and reliability. :: Test Bluetooth loss and intermittent service. For Tag Along and route reports, review consent, verification, misuse and stale information.
note: What I’d test in a pilot :: Compare phone-only and ring-triggered tasks. Record first-attempt activation without coaching, accidental sends, successful cancellations and time to confirmed receipt. Compare outcomes by commute context and connectivity. Treat every unrecognised delivery failure as a blocker to resolve.
small: Scope at handoff · Ring form concept, Figma designs and an interactive app walkthrough. Physical hardware, operational alerts and field outcomes remain to be validated.
