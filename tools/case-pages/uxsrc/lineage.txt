# Understanding the problem || One small shadow with several consequential decisions.
p: A designer working on a project wants an older shadow. However, the current photograph, background and wording need to stay.
p: In the Serein demo scenario, a manually refined shadow was approved in an earlier poster. A later AI-assisted edit made it heavier. Meanwhile, other parts of the poster changed. Restoring the whole earlier poster would also replace work the designer wants to keep.
small: PS: Serein is fictional. Its collaborators, comments, approvals and AI outputs are purely demonstration records. The scenario exercises a design hypothesis; it is not a client incident or a participant story.
table: Part of the poster | Earlier approval context | Current state
tr: Product photograph | photo-r2 | photo-r2 - unchanged
tr: Background | bg-r1 | bg-r3 - keep this
tr: Shadow + mask | shadow-r2 + mask-r1 | shadow-r3 + mask-r2 - replace together
tr: Typography | Earlier wording | Current wording - keep this
h: The problem I chose to solve
p: The aim of this product is to help someone **identify** the right recorded element, **understand** what its approval covered, and **predict** what recovering it will change.
p: **Selective restoration already exists. The open UX question is whether bringing this decision context together makes recovery clearer.**
h: An example of what a successful recovery must preserve
list: The chosen shadow must return with the mask it needs.
list: The current photograph, background and typography must remain intact.
list: Earlier versions and approvals must stay in the record.
list: The new result must be clearly marked for review.
note: Two terms to know: :: A **revision** is a saved version of one element. A **mask** controls where artwork is visible. Here the shadow relies on a particular mask, so recovering one without the other could change its appearance.

[P1] | Retain selective recovery as a question worth exploring, and include competent existing workarounds. | Small 2015 study; not current demand evidence for agencies.
tr: **Creative version control** 18 interviews explored how practitioners use versions as reusable material. [P2] | Organize around a selected element and its reusable revisions. | Cross-disciplinary sample and not an AI campaign-team study.
tr: **HistoryPalette** Prior research supports previewing and reusing earlier image alternatives in place. [P3] | Narrow the concept to approval scope, required assets and preservation information. | Close prior art plus in-place history reuse is not a new Invention.
tr: **Provenance guidance** A history record does not settle whether an assertion is true. [P6] | Keep recorded facts, collaborator declarations and unknowns separate. | No C2PA verification is implemented in this prototype.
note: Reasonable conclusion :: The evidence makes this a credible design investigation. It does not tell me how often small agencies face the scenario, how costly it is, or whether they would adopt a separate recovery workspace.

# Empathise || Designing for the job
p: Two proto-personas describe the work and the approval relationship.
h: Primary proto-persona - The campaign designer
table: Context | Design implication
tr: Produces campaign visuals in a small agency or studio, mixing imported artwork, manual edits and AI-assisted outputs. | Make selection visual and direct. Keep the current poster visible during comparison.
tr: Needs to recover an earlier element while retaining later improvements elsewhere. | List what changes and what stays before applying a recovery.
tr: May not have complete records of how every asset was made. | Expose gaps rather than inventing origin, authorship or tool information.
h: Secondary proto-persona - The creative lead or reviewer
table: Context | Design implication
tr: Reviews work created by someone else and needs to know what was approved. | Attach the reviewer, date, comment and composition context to the relevant revision.
tr: May encounter the same element in a changed composition. | Keep the earlier approval in its original context. Mark a recovered copy Needs review.
h: The shared job to be done
note: :: When I need an earlier approved element, help me identify it and see the effects of recovering it, so I can keep later work and make an informed choice.

# Define || Turning uncertainty into design questions.
quote: The “How might we?” question :: How might we help a designer recover a previously approved element while understanding its dependencies, evidence gaps and effects on the current composition?
table: Supporting question | Response in the prototype | Evidence status
tr: How might we show an older element in today's poster? | Compare the chosen revision in the current composition without saving. | Informed by history-reuse research; implementation checked.
tr: How might we prevent an old approval from looking like a new one? | Show the earlier approval context and require review of the recovered revision. | Project hypothesis; comprehension untested.
tr: How might we reveal an invisible dependency? | Explain that the shadow and its mask must be copied together. | Informed by dependency concerns; rule checked.
tr: How might we remain useful when evidence is incomplete? | Show unknown fields, name the block reason and provide an honest next step. | Evidence-boundary principle; usefulness untested.
h: Three principles that guided the trade-offs
ol: **Make scope visible.** The designer should be able to say what changes and what stays.
ol: **Keep history accountable.** New work gets a new revision; earlier approvals and records remain attached to their sources.
ol: **Prefer an explicit stop to a silent substitution.** If required artwork is missing or incompatible, explain why recovery cannot proceed.

# Ideate || Explaining and verifying four ways back.
table: Approach | Appeal | Reason for the decision
tr: Restore the whole poster | A familiar file-level rollback. | Too broad for this task as the designer wants the latest background, photograph and wording.
tr: Replay earlier operations | Could reconstruct a state from its edit history. | Requires reliable, complete operations and a consistent account of their dependencies. This prototype does not promise that.
tr: Ask AI to recreate the old shadow | Could produce a visually similar result. | Similarity cannot establish that it is the stored, previously reviewed artwork. It would need to be treated as new generation.
tr: **Copy the recorded snapshot** Chosen | Uses known stored artwork and creates a new, inspectable revision. | Fits the bounded task and requires available source material and compatible dependencies. Otherwise, it must refuse.
h: The cost of the chosen approach
p: The prototype cannot automatically recover separable elements from flattened or unrecorded artwork. Its fallback is to choose another recorded revision or redo the work manually in the editor. It explicitly says that this prototype has no branch editor.
h: Scope was also a design decision
p: I kept one end-to-end poster scenario:
list: Three selectable elements
list: Revision inspection, comparison
list: Recovery preview, apply, undo and reproducible failure states.
list: The mask remains a dependency rather than a fourth primary element and typography is the protected content.
small: PS:Live AI generation, accounts, multiplayer editing, cross-app capture and broad rights management were deferred. The prototype demonstrates the recovery decision, not a full creative suite.

# Information architecture || Choosing the piece. Reading its story.
table: Area | User question | Why it is here
tr: Elements list | Which part am I working on? | Three explicit choices also provide a keyboard alternative to clicking the poster.
tr: Composition canvas | What does it look like in today's poster? | Keeps the surrounding design visible when an earlier revision is compared.
tr: Revision inspector | What changed, what is known and what was approved? | Opens one revision card at a time to limit competing detail.
flow: 1 Select and inspect | Choose an element, then open a revision and read its recorded history.
flow: 2 Compare in context | View the earlier shadow inside the current poster. Nothing is saved.
flow: 3 Preview the consequences | Read what changes, what stays, what must travel together and what needs review.
flow: 4 Recover or keep the current version | Apply creates new revisions and a receipt. Cancel keeps the saved scene unchanged.
flow: 5 Undo if compatible | Create another revision that restores the previous appearance without deleting the recovery.

# Solution: inspect || “Not recorded” is a useful answer.
p: Selecting Shadow exposes its revisions, actor, time, operation, recorded comments and linked mask.
p: The current revision records an AI-assisted edit, while the tool name was not captured.
figs: 02 | Actual evidence block from the supplied standalone demo. The tool name remains visibly unrecorded.
h: Why three evidence groups?
list: **Recorded by the system** identifies captured metadata.
list: **Declared by a collaborator** identifies a person's statement.
list: **Unknown** identifies what the record cannot answer.
p: This avoids turning a collaborator's declaration into a verified fact, or an absent tool name into a guess about how the artwork was made.
h: Why the mask is not another main selection
p: The user is trying to recover a shadow. The mask matters because that task depends on it. Showing the mask as required supporting material preserves the relationship without asking the designer to manage a separate top-level recovery.

# Solution: approval || Approving has an address. It stays there.
p: The older shadow was reviewed alongside an earlier background and wording. Reusing the same artwork in a new composition changes that context.
figs: 03 | The approval block names the reviewer and date, shows the earlier poster and compares its context with today's.
rows: Decision | Keep the original approval attached to the original revision. Every recovered copy is a new revision marked Needs review.
rows: Why show then versus now? | A context table makes the difference inspectable. A date and a green badge alone would not explain what the reviewer saw.
rows: Accepted cost | Even an exact copy needs another review. This is conservative and may create overhead if a team uses a different approval model.

# Solution: compare and preview || Trying it on. Then reading the consequences.
p: The comparison places the earlier shadow inside today's poster, using explicit controls rather than hover. A framed canvas and “Preview only, nothing is saved” message identify the temporary state. The saved composition remains unchanged.
figs: 04 | The recovery preview shows the current and proposed compositions, plus enlarged shadow details. The marked area is the only visual difference.
table: Preview block | Question it answers
tr: Will change | Which shadow and mask revisions will be replaced?
tr: Will stay | Will the photograph, background and typography remain as they are?
tr: Required together | Why does the mask have to come with the shadow?
tr: After recovering: Needs review | Does the earlier approval carry forward? No.
tr: What is not known | Which evidence gaps remain unresolved?
h: Why enlarge the shadow?
p: Two complete posters fit in the dialog, but the small difference was difficult to inspect. The enlarged detail makes the actual decision visible without asking the user to infer it from metadata.

# Solution: recover and undo || Recovering gets a receipt. Undoing leaves a record.
p: Applying recovery copies the chosen shadow and its required mask into new revisions. It preserves the current photograph, background and typography; the original approval stays put.
p: The receipt lists the new revisions, their sources and the save time. Success appears only after the save succeeds, and the result is marked Needs review.
figs: 05 | Actual recovery receipt. Its timestamp reflects this walkthrough; it is not a launch date
table: State | Shadow / mask | History and approval
tr: Before recovery | shadow-r3 / mask-r2 | Earlier approved shadow-r2 remains available.
tr: After recovery | New shadow-r4 / new mask-r3 | Copied from shadow-r2 / mask-r1. New result needs review.
tr: After undo | New shadow-r5 / new mask-r4 | Restores the prior appearance. The recovery is still recorded.
h: Why undo adds a version
p: A whole-scene rollback could remove unrelated later changes. Here undo adds a compensating revision: another saved version that restores the previous shadow and mask. The history grows rather than losing the recovery event.
p: The cost is an unfamiliar meaning of undo and a longer history. If the shadow, its mask or the required photograph position changed incompatibly, undo is blocked instead of overwriting that newer work.

# Solution: difficult states || The “no” states are part of the design.
table: Situation | Behaviour | Why it matters
tr: Source artwork unavailable | Flag the unavailable source and block recovery and suggest another recorded revision or manual redo. | A record is not the same thing as recoverable artwork.
tr: Required mask missing | Block the recovery rather than silently using today's mask. | The result should match the stored combination.
tr: Photograph position incompatible | Block the shadow recovery and do not quietly restore the photograph. | The designer explicitly wanted the current photograph kept.
tr: Poster, approval or evidence changes after preview | Require a fresh preview before applying. | The designer should act on the state they actually reviewed.
tr: Saving fails | Show an error, preserve the previous state and show no success. | An unsaved change must not look complete.
tr: Undo would overwrite newer dependent work | Block undo and offer a new recovery instead. | Undo should not destroy later work to fulfil an earlier promise.
h: A deliberately conservative preview rule
p: Any change to the recorded scene invalidates an open preview, even when the change looks unrelated to the shadow. A more selective rule could reduce interruptions, but would need a second, reliable account of which changes affect the plan.

# Iterate || The screen challenged the specification.
p: These iterations came from implementation and visual review. They were not findings from a user test.
table: Observed problem | Change | Reason / trade-off
tr: A hidden-state check passed in a simulated page, while a CSS rule made the real interface visibly wrong. | Check the rendered application in a browser as well as the underlying rules. | A correct attribute does not guarantee the person sees the correct state.
tr: Two full posters made the shadow difference too small to inspect in the dialog. | Add enlarged shadow details alongside the complete compositions. | More vertical space, but a readable comparison of the thing being recovered.
tr: The earlier UI used nested frames, multiple resting shadows, uppercase labels and 14px body text. | A documented later design pass used space and tone, 16px body text and a full-width demo notice. | Reduce competing emphasis so the poster and the decision carry the hierarchy.
tr: Making the page behind the modal inactive broke focus restoration on Escape. | Remove the inactive state before returning focus to the invoking control. | An accessibility improvement introduced another accessibility problem; existing browser checks caught it.
h: What I left open
list: The revision card may be too long.
list: The approval wording may be too subtle.
list: Undo may not match expectations.
p: I recorded these concerns rather than writing a successful user outcome around them. The next study should establish where people hesitate and what they believe happened.

# Lineage / 14 / Accessibility || Recovery should not require a mouse.
table: Interaction need | Implemented / checked | Remaining limitation
tr: Choose the element | An explicit Elements list supports keyboard selection. Selected states are exposed programmatically. | The poster layers themselves have no direct keyboard path. The list is the alternative.
tr: Operate a dialog | Focus enters the dialog, remains inside, closes on Escape and returns appropriately. | No screen-reader walkthrough has been run.
tr: Understand the result | Status messages and words such as Needs review, Will change and Unavailable supplement colour. | Live-region presence is not proof of useful assistive-technology announcements.
tr: Read at a larger scale | The evaluation recorded no horizontal overflow at five widths, including 720px and 390px. | Real devices, long content and high-contrast mode were not tested.
tr: Avoid unnecessary motion | Transitions reduce to zero with the system's reduced-motion preference. | This is a specific check, not an accessibility-conformance result.
tr: See focus and text | Measured 3px focus outlines and automated rendered-text contrast checks. | Poster artwork text was excluded from the contrast scan.
h: Issues identified, still to address
list: The canvas hint leads with clicking.
list: The demo controls interrupt the keyboard route from element selection to history.
list: Reset is the first tab stop, with no skip link.
note: Accessibility status :: Next: a keyboard-only walkthrough by a person, followed by a screen-reader session with actual announcements recorded. The current evidence does not establish WCAG conformance.
